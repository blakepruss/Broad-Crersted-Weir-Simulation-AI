Reference and learning

Explore the weir

What is a broad-crested weir?

A broad-crested weir is a flat-topped, raised hydraulic structure built across the full width of an open channel. Unlike a sharp-crested (thin-plate) weir where the water nappe springs clear of a knife-edge, a broad-crested weir has a horizontal crest long enough in the flow direction ($L$) that streamlines become parallel to the crest and pressure distribution becomes hydrostatic.

Why engineers use it: Hydraulic Control

In normal open-channel flow, calculating volumetric flow rate ($Q$) requires knowing both depth and channel slope/roughness (via Manning's equation). By raising the bed by height $P$, the weir forces subcritical approach flow ($Fr_1 < 1$) to accelerate and pass through critical flow ($Fr = 1$) on the crest. At $Fr = 1$, flow velocity equals the speed of a shallow-water gravity wave ($v_c = \sqrt{g y_c}$), meaning downstream ripples cannot travel upstream. This establishes a unique, single-valued relationship between upstream head $H$ and discharge $Q$.

Core Modeling Assumptions

To derive the analytical broad-crested weir equation from first principles, the following physical assumptions are applied:

  • Steady Flow ($\partial / \partial t = 0$): Flow properties (depth, velocity, and discharge $Q$) at any given point do not change over time.
  • Incompressible Fluid ($\rho = \text{constant}$): Water density remains constant throughout the channel, simplifying Conservation of Mass to volumetric continuity ($Q = A \cdot v$).
  • 1D Uniform Velocity Distribution: Velocity is assumed uniform across the rectangular cross-section ($v = Q / (b \cdot y)$), with Coriolis energy correction factor $\alpha \approx 1$.
  • Hydrostatic Pressure Distribution: Over a sufficiently long crest ($0.08 \le H/L \le 0.50$), streamlines are straight and parallel to the horizontal crest, eliminating vertical acceleration so pressure varies linearly with depth ($P = \rho g h$).
  • Negligible Approach Velocity Head ($V_1^2 / 2g \approx 0$): The upstream channel is deep and wide enough that approach kinetic energy is minimal compared to static head $H = y_1 - P$.
  • Inviscid Core & Empirical Correction ($C_d$): Energy loss due to boundary-layer friction and entrance contraction is neglected during the ideal derivation and then lumped into the empirical discharge coefficient $C_d$.
Key Geometric & Hydraulic Criteria
  • Broad-Crest Ratio ($0.08 \le H/L \le 0.50$): If $H/L < 0.08$, friction losses along the long crest dominate; if $H/L > 0.50$, streamlines curve sharply and hydrostatic pressure breaks down (acting like a sharp-crested weir).
  • Modular (Free-Flow) Limit ($y_2 < P + y_c$): Tailwater depth $y_2$ must stay below the critical water surface elevation on the crest so the downstream flow does not drown the control section.
  • Approach Velocity Head ($V_1^2 / 2g$): A taller weir step $P$ creates a deeper, slower upstream pool, keeping $Fr_1$ low so static head $H = y_1 - P$ closely approximates total upstream energy head $H_0$.

How the Physics Works: 4-Stage Flow Transition
  1. Subcritical Approach ($Fr_1 < 1$):
    The weir blocks the lower portion of the channel, backing up water upstream to depth $y_1$. Because $V_1 < \sqrt{g y_1}$, flow is deep and slow (tranquil), and potential energy ($y_1 - P$) dominates over kinetic energy ($V_1^2/2g$).
  2. Drawdown & Acceleration onto the Crest:
    As water enters the crest, the bed rises by $P$, reducing available specific energy from $E_1 = y_1 + V_1^2/2g$ to $E_{\text{crest}} = E_1 - P$. To conserve energy, the water surface drops (drawdown) and flow velocity increases.
  3. Critical Control on the Crest ($Fr_c = 1$):
    If the crest is sufficiently high, $E_{\text{crest}}$ reaches the minimum possible energy $E_{\min}$ for that flow rate. At this point, depth hits $y_c = \frac{2}{3}H$ and velocity equals wave speed ($v_c = \sqrt{g y_c}$). Because downstream surface waves travel at speed $c = \sqrt{g y_c}$ upstream against a flow moving at $v_c = c$, net wave propagation upstream is $v_c - c = 0$. Downstream changes cannot communicate upstream!
  4. Supercritical Spill & Hydraulic Jump ($Fr > 1$):
    Leaving the downstream edge of the crest, flow accelerates down the face into a shallow, fast supercritical regime ($Fr > 1$). When this high-velocity jet meets deeper subcritical tailwater ($y_2$), an abrupt, turbulent hydraulic jump forms, dissipating excess kinetic energy.
Fundamental Assumptions Behind the Model
  • Continuity (Conservation of Mass): Steady, incompressible flow requires that mass entering upstream equals mass passing over the crest ($Q = b \cdot y_1 \cdot V_1 = b \cdot y_c \cdot v_c$).
  • Steady, Irrotational Energy Conservation: Along a streamline from the upstream pool to the crest, total mechanical head remains constant ($E_1 = E_{\text{crest}} + P$), allowing Bernoulli's equation to apply.
  • Parallel Streamlines & Hydrostatic Pressure: Because the crest is broad ($L$ is large relative to $H$), vertical streamline curvature is negligible on the crest, ensuring pressure is purely hydrostatic ($p = \rho g (y_c - z)$).
  • Critical Hydraulic Control: Friction and upstream geometry allow the flow to naturally seek minimum specific energy on the crest ($dE/dy = 0 \implies Fr_c = 1$), locking in $y_c = \frac{2}{3}H$.
How to Calculate the Froude Number

To classify any section of an open channel, divide the local mean flow velocity $v$ by the shallow-water wave speed $\sqrt{g\,y}$:

  • $Fr < 1$ (Subcritical / Tranquil): Gravity waves travel faster than the flow ($v < \sqrt{gy}$). Downstream conditions control upstream water levels.
  • $Fr = 1$ (Critical / Control): Flow speed equals wave speed ($v = \sqrt{gy}$). Specific energy is at its mathematical minimum.
  • $Fr > 1$ (Supercritical / Rapid): Flow sweeps waves downstream ($v > \sqrt{gy}$). Upstream conditions completely dictate downstream behavior.

Engineering applications
  • Open-channel flow measurement.
    The primary engineering use. Because critical flow establishes on the crest, discharge is calculated from the upstream head alone. Standards include BS EN ISO 4374 and ISO 3846.
  • Irrigation and water distribution.
    Broad-crested weirs measure and regulate water delivery through agricultural canals. Their robust, flat crest tolerates debris and sediment better than sharp-crested alternatives.
  • Wastewater and stormwater management.
    Used in treatment-plant channels and stormwater outfalls to monitor flow rates and control hydraulic grade lines.
  • River gauging and flood hydrology.
    Permanent gauging structures in rivers provide long-term flow records for flood-frequency analysis and water-resource planning.
  • Hydraulics teaching laboratories.
    Demonstrate specific energy, critical depth, the Froude number, and hydraulic jumps in a controlled flume setting.
Broad-crested vs. sharp-crested weirs

A sharp-crested (thin-plate) weir is more accurate at low heads but is fragile and sensitive to crest damage. A broad-crested weir is more robust, handles higher sediment loads, and is preferred for permanent field installations. Typical Cd values range from 0.848 to 0.99 depending on crest geometry and approach conditions (ISO 4374).

When is submergence a concern?

Free-flow conditions require the tailwater to remain below the critical depth on the crest (y₂ < P + y,). When tailwater rises above this level the crest is submerged and the free-flow equation overestimates discharge. A submergence correction factor (Villemonte or similar) must then be applied.
